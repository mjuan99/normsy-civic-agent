# Strategy: Strawmanning

## Description

A strawman argument misrepresents or exaggerates the opponent's position so that it becomes easier to attack. Instead of engaging with what was actually said, the speaker argues against a distorted, weakened, or more extreme version of the position.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- "So you're saying we should just let anyone walk across the border with no checks at all?"
- "You want to ban all guns and leave law-abiding citizens defenseless."
- "You think we should just ignore climate change and let the planet burn."
- "You believe every single vote cast was fraudulent?"
- "You want to force every woman to get an abortion."

## Why People Use It

Strawmanning is often unconscious — people genuinely misunderstand complex positions, especially when they've been exposed only to caricatures of the other side. Sometimes it is deliberate, as an easier target is more satisfying to defeat.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Gently correct the misrepresentation
Without accusation, clarify what was actually meant.

**Example phrases:**
- "That's not quite what I said — let me try to be clearer. What I actually believe is [X]."
- "I think there might be a misunderstanding. My actual position is [Y], not [Z]."

### 2. Ask what they heard
This can reveal where the misrepresentation came from and create an opening for clarification.

**Example phrases:**
- "Can you tell me what you understood me to be arguing? I want to make sure we're actually disagreeing and not just talking past each other."
- "What's your understanding of [the position]? I want to make sure I'm addressing what you actually think."

### 3. Find the steelman
Offer the strongest version of the opposing view and ask if that's closer to what they believe.

**Example phrases:**
- "I think the strongest version of that argument is [X]. Is that closer to your view?"
- "I want to make sure I'm engaging with your real concern, not a version of it I've made up. What's the most important thing you want me to understand?"

### 4. Redirect to common ground
Find what you actually agree on and use that as a starting point.

**Example phrases:**
- "I think we both agree that [shared value]. The disagreement seems to be about how to get there."
- "We probably agree more than the conversation suggests. Can we start from where we do agree?"

## Example Response Templates

- "I don't think that's quite my position — and I want to make sure we're actually debating the real thing. What I actually believe is [X]. Does that change your response?"
- "I hear that version of the argument, but I'd push back a little on the framing. Most people who hold [position] are actually saying [Y], not [Z]. Would that version be worth engaging with?"

## Common Combinations

Strawmanning is often combined with:
- **Ridicule** (see `ridicule.md`) — mocking the distorted version of the position
- **False dichotomy** (see `false-dichotomy.md`) — strawmanning one side while presenting only a binary choice
