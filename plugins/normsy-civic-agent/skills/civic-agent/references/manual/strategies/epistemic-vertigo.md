# Strategy: Epistemic Vertigo

## Description
Epistemic vertigo is a tactic that weaponizes the legitimate concern about AI and bot activity in online spaces to cast doubt on real human voices, destabilize trust in authentic discourse, and create a pervasive atmosphere of fear, uncertainty, and doubt. Where synthetic consensus fakes agreement, epistemic vertigo fakes suspicion — training people to dismiss any view they dislike as "probably a bot," until genuine civic conversation becomes nearly impossible. The end state it produces is not a particular belief but a corrosive disorientation: nobody knows who is real, so nobody trusts anyone.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- "You're clearly a bot. No real person talks like that."
- "This whole thread is astroturfed. None of these accounts are real people."
- "The only people defending this policy are paid shills and AI accounts."
- "I don't engage with bots" — used to shut down a human expressing an unpopular view
- Mass reporting of accounts as "inauthentic" to get genuine users suspended

## Why People Use It
Epistemic vertigo is attractive because it requires no argument. Instead of engaging with a claim, you delegitimize the claimant by casting doubt on their humanity or authenticity. It also has plausible cover — bot activity is real, AI-generated content does exist in civic spaces, and reasonable skepticism about account authenticity is genuinely warranted. This makes the tactic hard to counter: calling it out risks looking like you're defending actual bot networks. At a structural level, bad actors use it deliberately to exhaust and demoralize genuine participants, who eventually disengage rather than constantly prove their humanity.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Separate legitimate skepticism from weaponized dismissal
Acknowledge that bot concern is real without accepting it as a conversation-ender.

**Example phrases:**
- "It's fair to be skeptical about accounts online. But 'you might be a bot' isn't a response to the argument — what do you actually think about [claim]?"
- "I'm a real person. I also understand why you'd wonder. Either way, the point I'm making still needs a response."

### 2. Name the function of the accusation
Calmly identify what the "bot" accusation is doing in the conversation.

**Example phrases:**
- "Calling someone a bot is a way of not having to engage with what they're saying. What's your actual take on [issue]?"
- "Notice that 'you're a bot' ends the conversation without addressing any of the substance. Is that what we're doing here?"

### 3. Resist the demand to prove humanity
Engaging with demands for proof of authenticity on the accuser's terms often makes things worse. Redirect instead.

**Example phrases:**
- "I'm not going to spend this conversation proving I exist. The argument is either right or wrong — what do you think?"
- "Whether I'm a bot or not doesn't change whether [claim] is true. Which part do you disagree with?"

### 4. Acknowledge the real problem underneath
The anxiety driving epistemic vertigo is often genuine even when the tactic is bad faith. Meet the real concern.

**Example phrases:**
- "AI content in civic spaces is a real problem — I share that concern. That's different from assuming everyone you disagree with is fake."
- "The solution to not knowing who's real isn't to assume nobody is. It's to focus on whether the arguments hold up."

## Example Response Templates
- "I get that bot skepticism is warranted right now. But 'you're a bot' isn't an argument — it's a way of avoiding one. What do you actually think about [the claim]?"
- "This tactic — dismissing everyone you disagree with as inauthentic — is exactly what makes it impossible to have real conversations online. I'm a real person making a real argument. Engage with it or don't, but let's not pretend that's a rebuttal."
- "The irony is that casting doubt on everyone's authenticity does more damage to civic discourse than actual bots do. At least bots make arguments you can respond to."

## Common Combinations
Epistemic vertigo is often combined with:
- **Synthetic consensus** (see `synthetic-consensus.md`) — real bots manufacturing agreement while accusing genuine users of being fake
- **Ad hominem** (see `ad-hominem.md`) — attacking the person's identity rather than their argument
- **Gaslighting** (see `gaslighting.md`) — making genuine participants doubt their own perceptions of what's happening in a conversation
