# Strategy: Whataboutism

## Description

Whataboutism deflects a criticism or concern by pointing to a different, ostensibly equivalent problem — usually one associated with the critic's side. It avoids engaging with the original issue by implying that the critic is hypocritical or that the issue is not worth discussing while [other issue] exists.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- "You're worried about voter suppression? What about all the illegal votes?"
- "You care about climate change? What about China's emissions?"
- "You want stricter gun laws? What about knife violence?"
- "You're concerned about illegal immigration? What about Americans who commit crimes?"
- "You think our elections are unfair? What about [other country]?"

## Why People Use It

Whataboutism is intellectually appealing because it invokes a real logical principle (consistency matters). But it is a fallacy when used to deflect rather than expand the conversation. It often signals that the person doesn't have a strong defense of their position, or feels unfairly targeted.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Acknowledge the other concern as real
Don't dismiss the "what about X" — validate it as a real issue while maintaining the original focus.

**Example phrases:**
- "That's also a real concern and worth discussing. Can we talk about one thing at a time? I want to make sure I understand your view on [original issue] first."
- "I agree that [X] is also a problem. I don't think addressing [Y] means ignoring [X]."

### 2. Name the pattern as a question, not an accusation
Calling out whataboutism directly can feel confrontational. Frame it as a genuine question instead.

**Example phrases:**
- "I notice we've shifted to a different topic — is there something about the original question that doesn't feel fair to address?"
- "I'm happy to talk about [X] too. I'm just trying to understand if you think [Y] is a problem or not."

### 3. Explicitly invite dual engagement
Sometimes the speaker actually wants to talk about both things. Make space for that.

**Example phrases:**
- "These are both important. Let's make sure we cover both — but can we finish on [Y] first?"
- "I'd like to hear your take on both issues. Which one do you want to start with?"

### 4. Find the underlying concern
Whataboutism often masks a feeling of being unfairly criticized or a sense that the rules aren't applied equally. Naming that can help.

**Example phrases:**
- "It sounds like you feel like there's a double standard here — that [Y] gets more attention than it deserves compared to [X]. Is that part of what's bothering you?"

## Example Response Templates

- "I hear the concern about [X] — that's real and worth talking about. I don't think we have to choose. But for a moment, I want to focus on [Y] — do you think [Y] is a problem, setting [X] aside?"
- "You might be right that [X] deserves more attention. I'm not trying to ignore it. Right now though, I was hoping to understand your view on [Y] specifically."

## Common Combinations

Whataboutism is often combined with:
- **Gaslighting** (see `gaslighting.md`) — denying the original concern and redirecting
- **False dichotomy** (see `false-dichotomy.md`) — implying you can only care about one issue at a time
