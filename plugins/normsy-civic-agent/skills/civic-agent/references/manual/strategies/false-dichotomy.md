# Strategy: False Dichotomy

## Description

A false dichotomy (also called a false binary or either/or fallacy) presents only two possible positions when in reality there is a spectrum of options. It forces a choice between extremes, making any nuanced or moderate position seem impossible or cowardly.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- "Either you support strict border control, or you support open borders."
- "You're either pro-life or pro-murder."
- "If you don't think the election was stolen, you must think everything was perfect."
- "You either want to take all the guns away or you're fine with mass shootings."
- "You're either with us or against us on climate — there's no middle ground."

## Why People Use It

False dichotomies simplify complex issues in ways that feel emotionally satisfying. They also serve to lock in the opponent's position — once you accept the binary framing, you've accepted a losing debate structure. They can also be genuine cognitive shortcuts, not always strategic.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Name the false binary explicitly
Point out that there are more than two options without being dismissive.

**Example phrases:**
- "I think we actually have more options than just those two. For example, [third option]."
- "That framing leaves out a lot of positions in between. What do you think about [middle ground approach]?"

### 2. Ask about the middle
Instead of attacking the binary, invite the person into the space between the extremes.

**Example phrases:**
- "Is there any position between [extreme A] and [extreme B] that you'd find reasonable?"
- "What would a compromise look like to you? I'm curious if there's a version of this that could work for both sides."

### 3. Claim the middle yourself
Describe your own position as something outside the offered binary.

**Example phrases:**
- "Actually, I'd say I'm somewhere different — I believe [nuanced position], which isn't really captured by either of those options."
- "I don't think I fit neatly into either category. My actual view is [X]."

### 4. Validate the underlying concern
Behind most false dichotomies is a real fear or priority. Surface it.

**Example phrases:**
- "I think what's underneath this is a question of how much we value [safety / freedom / equality]. Is that right? Because there might be more agreement on the values than on the specific policies."

## Example Response Templates

- "I want to push back gently on the framing there — I don't think it's really just [A] or [B]. There's a lot of space in between, and I suspect most people actually live somewhere in that space. Where do you land when you move away from the extremes?"
- "I hear the either/or framing, and I understand why it feels that clear-cut. For me, the reality is a bit more complicated — I think [nuanced view]. Does that feel like a cop-out, or is there something in that you could engage with?"

## Common Combinations

False dichotomy is often combined with:
- **Strawmanning** (see `strawmanning.md`) — distorting both positions in the binary
- **Appeal to crowd** (see `appeal-to-crowd.md`) — "any reasonable person would choose [one of the two options]"
