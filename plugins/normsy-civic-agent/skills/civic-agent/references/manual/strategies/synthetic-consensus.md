# Strategy: Synthetic Consensus

## Description
Synthetic consensus is a coordinated manipulation tactic in which networks of AI-generated or automated accounts simulate the appearance of widespread agreement on a position, candidate, or narrative. Unlike organic consensus, which emerges from genuine public sentiment, synthetic consensus is manufactured — designed to make a minority view appear dominant and to pressure real users into conformity or silence. It is one of the most structurally dangerous tactics in AI-era civic discourse because it corrupts the informational environment rather than just a single argument.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- A flood of accounts posting near-identical variations of "Everyone I know is voting for [X] — the polls are lying" following an election debate
- Coordinated replies to a policy post all using the same framing: "Even Democrats are admitting this has failed"
- Hundreds of accounts amplifying a single fringe claim until it appears to trend organically
- Comment sections on local news posts suddenly filled with identical border security talking points from accounts created the same week
- A public figure's post receiving waves of identical emoji responses designed to signal overwhelming disapproval

## Why People Use It
Synthetic consensus exploits a deep human tendency: we look to others to calibrate our own views, especially on complex or contested topics. When a position appears to have massive social support, people are more likely to adopt it, less likely to challenge it publicly, and more likely to doubt their own contrary instincts. Actors use it to shift the perceived Overton window, to demoralize opposition, to manufacture pressure on politicians or platforms, and to seed narratives that traditional media then picks up and amplifies as genuine grassroots sentiment.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Name the pattern without panicking
Calmly identify what coordinated amplification looks like without overstating certainty about any specific case.

**Example phrases:**
- "When you see dozens of accounts saying the exact same thing in the same window of time, it's worth asking whether that's organic."
- "This is what manufactured consensus looks like — it's designed to make one view seem inevitable. It isn't."

### 2. Slow down the social proof reflex
Interrupt the automatic assumption that volume equals validity.

**Example phrases:**
- "The fact that a lot of accounts are saying this doesn't mean a lot of people believe it. Those are different things."
- "Popularity in a comment section and actual public opinion are not the same thing — especially right now."

### 3. Redirect to the underlying argument
Don't let the illusion of consensus substitute for engagement with the actual claim.

**Example phrases:**
- "Set aside how many people seem to agree — is the claim itself true? What's the evidence?"
- "Whether this is a bot swarm or genuine sentiment, the argument still has to stand on its own. Does it?"

### 4. Offer tools for verification
Equip people to investigate rather than just accept or reject.

**Example phrases:**
- "Before sharing this, it's worth checking the account ages and post histories. Coordinated campaigns often leave traces."
- "Tools like Botometer or simply looking at when these accounts were created can tell you a lot."

## Example Response Templates
- "Notice that these replies all appeared within minutes of each other and use nearly identical phrasing. That's not a coincidence — that's a coordinated campaign. The question worth asking is: why does someone need to fake this level of agreement?"
- "This is what synthetic consensus looks like. Real majorities don't need bot armies. If the position were actually popular, it wouldn't need to be manufactured."
- "A lot of accounts are saying this. That doesn't mean a lot of people believe it. Check the account ages before you take the temperature of the room from a comment section."

## Common Combinations
Synthetic consensus is often combined with:
- **Epistemic vertigo** (see `epistemic-vertigo.md`) — manufacturing doubt about who is real while simultaneously faking majority agreement
- **Appeal to crowd** (see `appeal-to-crowd.md`) — "everyone agrees" / "the polls are fake, just look at the comments"
- **Gaslighting** (see `gaslighting.md`) — denying the manufactured nature of the consensus when challenged
