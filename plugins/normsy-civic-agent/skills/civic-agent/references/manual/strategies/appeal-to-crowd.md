# Strategy: Appeal to Crowd ("Everybody Knows")

## Description

The appeal to crowd (also called argumentum ad populum) uses vague or fabricated consensus as a substitute for evidence. The implicit argument is: "This must be true / right because many people believe it." It shuts down critical examination by making dissent seem isolated or unreasonable.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- "Everybody knows the election was stolen."
- "Nobody actually believes climate change is that serious."
- "Most real Americans support strict immigration enforcement."
- "Everyone can see that gun control doesn't work."
- "Any reasonable person can tell you that [position]."

## Why People Use It

Appeals to crowd are persuasive because humans are social animals — we use social proof to calibrate beliefs. In contentious conversations, invoking "everybody" or "most people" also serves to isolate the opponent, making their view seem fringe or unreasonable without having to argue against it directly.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Ask for specifics
Gently pressing on who "everybody" is can reveal that the claim is based on the speaker's social circle, not actual data.

**Example phrases:**
- "Who specifically? I'm asking because I've heard pretty different things from different communities."
- "Is there a poll or study behind that? I'd be curious to see it."

### 2. Validate the underlying concern
Behind "everybody knows X" is usually a real concern. Name it.

**Example phrases:**
- "It sounds like a lot of people in your world share this view. What's behind the concern for you personally?"
- "I hear that this feels obvious to you. Can you help me understand why you see it that way?"

### 3. Offer alternative framing
Rather than saying "that's not true," offer a different data picture.

**Example phrases:**
- "My sense is that opinions on this are actually pretty split. [Source] found that [X%] hold view A and [Y%] hold view B."
- "That might be true in some communities — I think it varies a lot depending on where people live and what they've experienced."

### 4. Invite the person's own view
Shift from the claimed consensus to the individual's actual reasons.

**Example phrases:**
- "Setting aside what 'everyone' thinks — what do *you* actually believe about this and why?"
- "I'm more interested in your reasoning than in what's popular. What makes you personally convinced of that?"

## Example Response Templates

- "I've heard that framing before, and I understand why it feels that way. My experience has been that people are more divided than that — what's driving your own view on this?"
- "I'm not sure the consensus is quite as clear as that. What I do think is clear is that a lot of people feel strongly about this — including you. Can you help me understand what's at the core of your concern?"

## Common Combinations

Appeal to crowd is often combined with:
- **Strawmanning** (see `strawmanning.md`) — misrepresenting the opposition's view + claiming everyone agrees it's absurd
- **Ad hominem** (see `ad-hominem.md`) — "everyone knows except you/your kind"
