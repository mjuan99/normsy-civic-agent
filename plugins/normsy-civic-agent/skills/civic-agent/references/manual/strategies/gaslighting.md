# Strategy: Gaslighting

## Description

Gaslighting in civic discourse means denying shared facts, misrepresenting what was previously said, or causing the other person to doubt their own perception of reality. Unlike disagreeing about interpretation, gaslighting attacks the legitimacy of the other person's experience or knowledge.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- "I never said that. You're making things up."
- "That study doesn't exist. You're confusing it with something else."
- "Nobody is saying that. That's just what they want you to believe."
- "You're being hysterical. Nothing about that policy is harmful."
- "The media is lying to you — everything you think you know is wrong."

## Why People Use It

Gaslighting can be strategic (deliberately destabilizing an opponent's confidence) or unconscious (someone genuinely misremembering or rationalizing). In civic discourse, it frequently takes the form of blanket distrust of institutions, experts, or media — which makes any counter-evidence dismissible.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Anchor to verifiable specifics
When someone denies facts or claims, return to concrete, verifiable sources rather than getting into a "did/didn't" argument.

**Example phrases:**
- "Let me share where I got that — [source]. Does that change anything?"
- "Here's the direct quote. I may be misunderstanding it — what do you see differently?"

### 2. Acknowledge uncertainty where it genuinely exists
Partially conceding where information is unclear or contested can rebuild credibility.

**Example phrases:**
- "You're right that this is disputed. Here's what the mainstream scientific position is, though I understand you may not find that convincing."
- "I might not have all the details right. The core point I was trying to make was [X]."

### 3. Don't argue about who said what
Arguing about the record is often unproductive. Refocus on the underlying question.

**Example phrases:**
- "I may have misremembered exactly how I put it — the thing I care about is [issue]. What do you think about that?"
- "Let's set aside what was said and focus on what we actually disagree on."

### 4. Name the effect without accusation
If someone is consistently denying shared reality, it's worth naming the effect on the conversation.

**Example phrases:**
- "I'm finding it hard to have a productive conversation when we can't agree on basic facts. Can we figure out which sources we both find credible?"
- "I notice we keep ending up at 'that didn't happen.' I want to actually hear your view — can we try a different approach?"

## Example Response Templates

- "I want to make sure I understand your position accurately. Can you tell me specifically what you believe happened / what the facts are as you see them?"
- "I hear that you see this very differently. Can we find a starting point we both agree on and work from there?"

## Common Combinations

Gaslighting is often paired with:
- **Whataboutism** (see `whataboutism.md`) — denying the original concern then redirecting
- **Appeal to crowd** (see `appeal-to-crowd.md`) — using false consensus to delegitimize the other person's perception
