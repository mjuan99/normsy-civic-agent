# Strategy: Ad Hominem

## Description

Ad hominem (Latin: "to the person") attacks the character, identity, motives, or personal traits of the person making an argument rather than addressing the argument itself. It is one of the most common and most corrosive attack types in civic discourse because it makes conversations personal and shuts down substantive engagement.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- "Of course you believe that — you're a liberal snowflake."
- "You only think that because you're brainwashed by Fox News."
- "You're just racist. That's why you care about immigration."
- "You only support that policy because you benefit from it personally."
- "Nobody should listen to you — you don't even vote."

## Why People Use It

Ad hominem attacks often emerge from frustration, tribalism, or a desire to delegitimize rather than engage. They can also stem from genuine moral concern — if someone believes a position is deeply wrong, attacking the person may feel justified. Sometimes it is a defensive move when the person doesn't have strong counter-arguments.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Decline to take it personally
Resist the urge to defend yourself or retaliate. Return the conversation to substance.

**Example phrases:**
- "I hear the frustration. I'd like to keep talking about [issue] if we can."
- "That stings a bit, honestly — but I'd rather focus on the actual question. What do you think about [X]?"

### 2. Separate the person from the position
Model the behavior you want by explicitly distinguishing between character and argument.

**Example phrases:**
- "Even if everything you said about me were true, it wouldn't change whether [argument] is correct. What do you think about the argument itself?"
- "I'm not going to try to convince you I'm a good person — that's probably not going to work in a text conversation. But I am interested in whether the argument holds up. Do you think [X]?"

### 3. Acknowledge the underlying attribution
Sometimes an ad hominem masks a legitimate concern about bias or motivation. Take it seriously.

**Example phrases:**
- "You might be right that my background shapes how I see this. I'd still like to hear what you think is wrong with the argument itself."
- "That's a fair question — I do have a perspective on this. Here's where I'm coming from: [brief, honest statement]. Does that address the concern?"

### 4. Name the pattern with compassion
If the attacks persist, name what's happening calmly.

**Example phrases:**
- "I notice we've moved from the issue to talking about me personally. I'd rather not go there — can we get back to [topic]?"
- "I don't want this to become about either of us as people. I think the question of [topic] matters more than whether either of us is a good person."

## Example Response Templates

- "I hear that you don't think much of where I'm coming from. That's fair — but I'm more interested in whether the argument is right than in whether you like me. What do you actually think about [the specific claim]?"
- "Let's set aside who we each are for a moment. The question on the table is [X]. Do you think [X] is true, and if not, why?"

## Common Combinations

Ad hominem is often combined with:
- **Ridicule** (see `ridicule.md`) — mocking both the person and the argument
- **Appeal to crowd** (see `appeal-to-crowd.md`) — "anyone with sense can see you're wrong"
