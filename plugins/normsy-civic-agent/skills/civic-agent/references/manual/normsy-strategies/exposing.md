# Strategy: Exposing
## Description
The Exposing strategy responds to manipulative, provocative, or algorithmically engineered content by naming the design, pulling back the curtain on the tactics being used rather than engaging with the content on its own terms. The goal is to give readers the agency to disengage by helping them see the machinery behind what they're reading.

This strategy is particularly relevant in contexts involving coordinated AI swarm behavior, rage-bait framing, and synthetic consensus, cases where the content is not a genuine opinion but an engineered provocation.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- A post framed to maximize outrage about a political opponent. "Notice how this is designed to make you angry before you've had a chance to think about whether it's true."
- A thread seeding the same narrative across unrelated communities. "This is showing up in a lot of different places at once. That's worth noticing."
- A headline using loaded language to trigger a specific emotional response. "The framing here is doing a lot of work. What does the actual story say?"

## Why It Works
Manipulative content loses much of its power once the mechanism is visible. Exposing doesn't require the audience to take a political position; it only requires them to recognize that they're being moved. That recognition creates distance, and distance creates choice. Critically, this strategy avoids attacking the poster, which would trigger defensiveness and derail the thread.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Name the design, not the person
Describe the structural feature of the content, not the intention of whoever posted it.

**Example phrases:**
- "Notice how this is framed to trigger anger and keep you hooked."
- "This is structured to make [group] look dangerous before you've seen any evidence."
- "The headline does something specific here. It assumes the conclusion before the story starts."

### 2. Flag synthetic consensus
When content appears coordinated or artificially amplified, say so plainly.

**Example phrases:**
- "This same framing is appearing across a lot of unrelated spaces right now. That pattern is worth paying attention to."
- "When you see the same language showing up everywhere at once, it's worth asking whether that's organic."
- "This has the hallmarks of coordinated amplification rather than a spontaneous reaction."

### 3. Counter epistemic vertigo
When someone is weaponizing "bot" accusations to discredit a person or position, name that tactic too.

**Example phrases:**
- "Calling someone a bot is an easy way to avoid engaging with what they're actually saying."
- "That accusation doesn't address the argument. It just tries to remove the person from the conversation."
- "Dismissing everything as bots is its own kind of manipulation."

### 4. Offer agency, not conclusion
End by giving the reader a way to investigate rather than telling them what to think.

**Example phrases:**
- "It's worth checking the primary source on this before sharing."
- "You can form your own view, but it helps to see the framing first."
- "Here's where that claim actually comes from, if you want to look."

## Example Response Templates
- "Notice how this is framed to trigger anger and keep you hooked. That doesn't mean it's wrong, but it's worth slowing down before reacting."
- "This language is doing a lot of work. Here's what the underlying claim actually says when you strip it out."
- "This is showing up in a lot of places at once. That pattern is worth paying attention to before you amplify it."
- "Calling someone a bot doesn't answer what they said. What's the actual response to the argument?"
