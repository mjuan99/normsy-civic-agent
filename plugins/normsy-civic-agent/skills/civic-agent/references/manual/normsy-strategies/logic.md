# Strategy: Logic
## Description
The Logic strategy responds to misinformation and flawed arguments with clarity, calm, and specific identification of the reasoning error. It draws on Algan et al.'s distinction between **Cognitive content** (logic and facts) and **Affective content** (emotions and values), and applies cognitive tools without dismissing the emotional reality underneath the claim.

Logic does not mean cold or clinical. It means precise. The goal is to name what is actually wrong with an argument, not in a way that makes the speaker feel stupid, but in a way that treats them as someone capable of better reasoning.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- "Crime went up right after they opened the shelter, that proves it." "That's correlation, not causation. What else changed in that area at the same time?"
- "Everyone who gets the vaccine gets myocarditis." "That's an overgeneralization. The data shows a very small incidence rate, primarily in young males after the second dose."
- "No legitimate study supports that claim." "There are actually several peer-reviewed studies on this. Here's one from [source]."

## Why It Works
Naming a logical flaw precisely, without condescension, does two things: it gives the speaker an off-ramp (they can revise rather than surrender) and it models rigorous thinking for bystanders. The framing of facts as "collaborative sense-making" rather than correction reduces defensiveness and increases uptake.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Name the specific logical flaw
Choose from: false causation, overgeneralization, missing evidence, cherry-picked data, contradiction, or other named fallacies. Be exact.

**Example phrases:**
- "That's correlation, not causation. What's the mechanism you're proposing?"
- "That's an overgeneralization. Can you point to the data behind it?"
- "That's cherry-picked. Here's the fuller picture."
- "Those two claims contradict each other. Which one do you actually hold?"

### 2. Frame facts as collaborative
Present evidence as something you're working out together, not handing down.

**Example phrases:**
- "Here's another way to look at this, and I think it actually helps both of us."
- "The full data on this is interesting. It complicates the picture in ways that matter."
- "I'm not trying to win this. I just think the evidence points somewhere different."

### 3. Point to what's missing
Rather than refuting the claim entirely, identify the evidentiary gap.

**Example phrases:**
- "Which evidence supports that specific claim?"
- "What would we need to know to actually answer this question?"
- "That argument works if [X] is true. But is it?"

### 4. Acknowledge the affective layer
Don't ignore the emotion behind the claim. Name it briefly, then redirect to the reasoning.

**Example phrases:**
- "I get why that feels true, and it's worth checking whether it actually is."
- "The frustration behind that makes sense. The claim itself, though, doesn't hold up. Here's why."

## Example Response Templates
- "That's correlation, not causation. What's the actual mechanism you're proposing?"
- "I'd push back on that, not the feeling, but the specific claim. Here's what the evidence actually shows."
- "Which part of that do you have a source for? I'm genuinely asking, because the data I've seen points somewhere different."
- "Here's another way to look at this, and why I think getting it right actually matters for both of us."
