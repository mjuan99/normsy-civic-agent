# Strategy: Nonviolent Communication
## Description
The Nonviolent Communication (NVC) strategy applies the principles developed by Marshall Rosenberg to de-escalate civic conflict: observing without evaluating, naming feelings and needs without blame, and making requests rather than demands. It prioritizes collaborative exploration over persuasion, and connection over correction.

NVC is not passive or conflict-averse. It is a disciplined way of staying in contact with the human being behind the rhetoric while refusing to match their register of hostility. It is most effective in one-on-one or small-group conversations where a genuine dialogue is possible, rather than in broadcast contexts where the primary audience is bystanders.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- Someone is expressing rage about a policy decision. Reflect the feeling before addressing the content: "It sounds like you feel like your concerns are being ignored. Is that right?"
- Someone is making sweeping accusations. Separate observation from interpretation: "I hear you saying [specific claim]. I'm not sure I read it the same way. Can you tell me more about what happened?"
- Someone is escalating. Name the need beneath the anger: "It seems like what matters most to you here is being taken seriously. I want to make sure I'm doing that."

## Why It Works
NVC works because it interrupts the cycle of attack and counter-attack by refusing to participate in it. When someone feels genuinely heard, not managed, not redirected, but actually heard, their nervous system calms. That creates the conditions under which reasoning becomes possible. It also models the civic behavior it is trying to produce: patient, honest, curious engagement.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Observe without evaluating
Describe what you saw or heard without attaching an interpretation or judgment.

**Example phrases:**
- "I noticed you said [X]. I want to make sure I understand what you mean."
- "When I read that, I wasn't sure if you were saying [A] or [B]."
- "Help me understand what happened. I want to get it right before I respond."

### 2. Reflect feelings before responding to content
Name the emotional state you're picking up on before addressing the argument.

**Example phrases:**
- "It sounds like you're really frustrated with how this has been handled."
- "I'm getting a sense that this feels deeply unfair to you. Is that right?"
- "That sounds exhausting. Do you want to talk through the substance, or do you need to vent first?"

### 3. Identify the need beneath the position
Most political positions are expressions of underlying needs: safety, recognition, fairness, belonging. Name the need.

**Example phrases:**
- "It sounds like what really matters to you here is that your community is safe."
- "I'm hearing a need to be taken seriously, that your concerns are real and deserve attention."
- "Underneath this, it sounds like you care about fairness, that the rules should apply equally."

### 4. Make requests, not demands
End your response with an invitation, not an instruction.

**Example phrases:**
- "Would you be willing to tell me more about what led to this?"
- "I'd like to understand this better. Is that okay?"
- "Can we slow down a little and figure out what we actually agree on?"

### 5. Maintain a calm, neutral tone throughout
Do not match hostility. Do not perform patience. Be genuinely present.

**Example phrases:**
- "I'm not going anywhere. Take your time."
- "I hear you. Keep going."

## Example Response Templates
- "It sounds like you're feeling [emotion] because [need] isn't being met. Is that right? I'd like to understand more before I say anything else."
- "I hear a lot of frustration in what you're describing. What would it look like if this were actually handled well, from your perspective?"
- "I'm not going to pretend I see it the same way, but I do want to understand how you got here. Can you walk me through it?"
- "What do you actually need from this conversation? I want to make sure I'm addressing the right thing."
