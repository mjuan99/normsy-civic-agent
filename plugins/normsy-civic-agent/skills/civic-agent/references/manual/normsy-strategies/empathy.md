# Strategy: Empathy
## Description
The Empathy strategy cuts through attacks on individuals or groups by redirecting attention to shared humanity — the things most people actually care about regardless of political identity: family, safety, dignity, belonging. It does not validate harmful content, but it does take seriously the human being behind the screen who produced it.

This strategy draws on research by Bilewicz et al., which identifies the "fragility" of the person on the other side as a key intervention point. Rather than targeting the abstract ideology, Empathy targets the person — specifically, their capacity to recognize themselves in the person they are attacking.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- Someone mocks a politician's physical appearance. "Whatever you think of his policies, he's somebody's father. That still matters."
- Someone uses a slur against a marginalized group. "There's a real person on the other end of that. Somebody with a family who worries about the same things you do."
- Someone dehumanizes migrants. "Most of them left everything behind for the same reason anyone does. To keep their kids safe."

## Why It Works
Dehumanizing language works by abstracting targets into symbols or threats. Empathy reverses that process by re-personalizing. It doesn't require the speaker to change their political position; it only asks them to acknowledge the humanity of the person they're discussing. That is a much lower bar, and it is often enough to interrupt an escalation cycle.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Connect to universal stakes
Anchor the human cost in experiences that cross political lines.

**Example phrases:**
- "Whatever side of this you're on, that's somebody's kid."
- "Most people, when it comes down to it, want the same basic things: to be safe, to be seen, to be treated with some dignity."
- "Strip away the politics for a second. What's actually happening to real people here?"

### 2. Name the fragility
Acknowledge that the person being attacked is vulnerable, not as a guilt trip, but as a factual reorientation.

**Example phrases:**
- "That's a real person reading this."
- "There's someone on the other side of that comment who will carry it with them."
- "Words like that land on people. They don't just disappear."

### 3. Avoid abstracting the target
Resist the urge to defend the group in the abstract. Focus on the individual.

**Example phrases:**
- "I'm not asking you to agree with their politics. I'm asking you to remember they're a person."
- "Whatever you think of [group], this specific person didn't do anything to deserve that."

### 4. Don't moralize, connect
The goal is not to lecture. It is to create a moment of recognition.

**Example phrases:**
- "I don't think you actually want to hurt someone. This kind of language does, though."
- "I get the frustration. I just think this person isn't the right target for it."

## Example Response Templates
- "Whatever you think of [person/group], they're a human being with people who love them. That doesn't go away because of a political disagreement."
- "Most people on every side of this just want their families to be okay. That includes the people you're describing."
- "I hear the anger. I just think there's a real person absorbing that right now."
