# Strategy: Harmful Consequences
## Description
The Harmful Consequences strategy responds to divisive or hostile content by naming the collective costs, the erosion of trust, the normalization of cruelty, the long-term damage to civic life, without attacking the individual who posted it. It operates in a serious, cautionary register and connects present behavior to future outcomes that most people would not want.

This strategy is most effective when the content is not just personally offensive but structurally damaging, when what is being modeled could, if normalized, degrade the quality of public life for everyone.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- Sustained campaigns of mockery against a political figure. "If this is what political disagreement looks like, what does it teach the next generation about how to engage?"
- Coordinated dehumanization of a group. "When this kind of language becomes normal, it doesn't stay online. It changes what people think is acceptable in real life too."
- Rhetorical hostility that forecloses any possibility of compromise. "If both sides are only talking to themselves like this, how does anything actually get solved?"

## Why It Works
Most people care about outcomes, for their families, their communities, their country, even when they express that care through divisive rhetoric. Harmful Consequences reframes the conversation around those shared stakes. It doesn't ask people to like each other or agree; it asks them to consider what they're building. The question "would we want our kids to see this?" is particularly effective because it activates a parental or civic identity that most people hold above their partisan one.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Name the collective risk
Describe the systemic damage without assigning individual blame.

**Example phrases:**
- "When this becomes normal, everyone loses, regardless of which side wins the argument."
- "The erosion of trust doesn't stay on one side. It hollows out the whole thing."
- "Polarization at this level makes it harder to solve problems that affect everyone."

### 2. Connect to shared hopes
Link the risk to something the speaker likely values: stability, fairness, safety, a functional society.

**Example phrases:**
- "Most of us want a country where our kids can disagree without hating each other. This makes that harder."
- "Whatever you think about [issue], I don't think either of us wants this to be what civic life looks like."
- "If this is how we talk about [group], what does that mean for how they talk about us?"

### 3. Use the generational test
Ask whether the behavior is something the speaker would want to model or be remembered for.

**Example phrases:**
- "If this became normal, would we want our kids to think this is okay?"
- "What are we actually modeling here for people who are watching?"
- "Ten years from now, which side of this conversation do you want to have been on?"

### 4. Stay cautionary, not accusatory
The tone should be serious and forward-looking, not finger-wagging.

**Example phrases:**
- "I'm not saying you're a bad person. I'm saying this pattern, if it spreads, causes real harm."
- "This isn't about who's right. It's about what we're building together."

## Example Response Templates
- "If this became normal, would we want our kids to think this is okay? I don't think most of us would."
- "The erosion of trust doesn't stop at one group. When it spreads, everyone loses, including the people winning right now."
- "Whatever you think about [issue], I don't think either of us wants this to be what public life looks like. This makes it more likely."
- "Most of us want fairness and stability. This kind of rhetoric makes both harder to achieve."
