# Strategy: Morality
## Description
The Morality strategy anchors responses to harmful or divisive rhetoric in civic and cultural ideals, fairness, honesty, compassion, mutual respect, rather than in political positions or factual corrections. It speaks values-first, appealing to the standards most people already hold even when their rhetoric violates them.

This strategy is most effective when the content is not primarily factual but normative, when what is being expressed is a failure of civic character rather than a factual error. It works by reminding speakers of who they want to be, not just what they should believe.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- Someone calls for violence against a political opponent. "We're better than this. Whatever the disagreement, calling for harm to another person is something most of us reject."
- Someone uses dehumanizing language about a religious group. "Fairness means the same rules apply to everyone. That includes how we talk about people we disagree with."
- Someone celebrates the suffering of a political figure. "Compassion doesn't stop at party lines. Most of us know that, even when we forget it."

## Why It Works
Morality-based interventions work because most people do not want to see themselves as unfair or cruel, even when their rhetoric is. By invoking a shared value rather than a political position, the Morality strategy gives the speaker a path back to their better self without requiring them to concede a political argument. It also signals to bystanders what civic norms look like in practice.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Lead with the value, not the violation
State the principle before describing how the content contradicts it.

**Example phrases:**
- "We owe each other basic dignity. That's not a political position, it's a civic one."
- "Honesty matters here, not because of who's right, but because we all lose when the truth gets buried."
- "Fairness means applying the same standard to everyone, including people we can't stand."

### 2. Invoke prescriptive norms
Remind the speaker of abstract civic ideals they are likely to already endorse.

**Example phrases:**
- "Most people, when they step back, believe we should be able to disagree without wishing harm on each other."
- "The standard we want others to meet, we have to be willing to meet it ourselves."
- "This is the kind of thing most of us wouldn't want our kids to see us post."

### 3. Separate the political from the moral
Make clear that the moral concern is independent of the political disagreement.

**Example phrases:**
- "You can think [X] is wrong without wanting [person] to suffer."
- "Disagreeing with someone's policy doesn't require dehumanizing them."
- "The argument against [position] doesn't need this kind of language to be right."

### 4. Keep it short and direct
Morality statements lose force when they become lectures. One or two sentences is usually enough.

**Example phrases:**
- "We're better than this."
- "That's not who most of us want to be."
- "We owe each other more than that."

## Example Response Templates
- "We're better than this. Whatever the disagreement, most of us don't actually want to live in a world where this is how we talk to each other."
- "Fairness means the same rules apply to everyone, including how we talk about people we disagree with."
- "You can make a strong argument against [position] without this. The argument doesn't need it."
- "Compassion doesn't stop at party lines. Most people know that, even when it's hard to practice."
