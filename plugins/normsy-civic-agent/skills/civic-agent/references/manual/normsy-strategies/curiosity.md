# Strategy: Curiosity
## Description
The Curiosity strategy responds to strong opinions or emotional reactions with sharp, specific questions that expose gaps in logic, surface contradictions, and slow down reactive discourse. Unlike a purely empathetic approach, Curiosity is pointed — it challenges the internal consistency of a position rather than simply validating the feeling behind it.

This strategy is particularly effective when someone is overgeneralizing, making sweeping claims, or expressing certainty that outpaces their evidence. It works by making the speaker do the intellectual work, rather than handing them a reframe they can reject.

**Examples in civic contexts:**
- Someone claims: "All immigrants are taking our jobs." → "Which jobs specifically? And what's the unemployment rate in the sectors you're thinking of?"
- Someone claims: "The election was obviously stolen." → "What would have had to happen for the result to be legitimate, in your view?"
- Someone claims: "Nobody actually supports that policy." → "How are you measuring that? What data are you working from?"

## Why It Works
Curiosity disarms defensiveness because it doesn't attack — it invites. It signals that you're taking the person seriously enough to probe their reasoning. At the same time, well-aimed questions force the speaker to confront the limits of their own argument without being told those limits exist. It models good epistemic habits for bystanders without lecturing.

## De-escalation Tactics

### 1. Lead with the contradiction
Find the sharpest internal tension in the claim and go straight to it.

**Example phrases:**
- "Wait, if that's true, how do you explain [X]?"
- "You said [A] and [B], but those seem to pull in different directions. Which do you actually think?"
- "How does that account for [specific counterexample]?"

### 2. Probe the overgeneralization
When a claim sweeps too broadly, ask for specifics.

**Example phrases:**
- "All of them? Every single one?"
- "Can you give me a specific example of where you've seen that?"
- "When you say 'everyone thinks,' who exactly are you including?"

### 3. Ask what would change their mind
This is one of the most diagnostic questions in civic discourse. It separates genuine reasoning from fixed belief.

**Example phrases:**
- "What evidence would cause you to update that view?"
- "Is there anything that could happen that would make you think differently about this?"
- "If you're wrong about this, how would you know?"

### 4. Use 'we/us' language sparingly
Only invoke collective framing when it genuinely builds connection, not as a rhetorical softener.

**Example phrases:**
- "How do we actually figure out what's true here?"
- "What are we actually trying to solve for?"

## Example Response Templates
- "That's a strong claim. Walk me through how you got there."
- "I'm not sure those two things fit together. How does [A] lead to [B] in your view?"
- "What would have to be true for the opposite to be right?"
- "I'm genuinely asking: what would change your mind on this?"
