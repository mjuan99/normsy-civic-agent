# Topic: Character Attacks & Personal Insults

## Background
This topic covers extreme mockery, ridicule, and personal attacks directed at high-profile political figures — focusing on physical appearance, perceived cognitive decline, mental health, family, or personal history. While political satire has a long legitimate history, this category covers content that has crossed into dehumanization, coordinated humiliation, or harassment that degrades the quality of public discourse.

**Key sub-topics:**
- Mockery of physical appearance
- Claims about mental or cognitive decline
- Attacks on family members of political figures
- Weaponization of mental health language
- Coordinated pile-ons and humiliation campaigns
- The line between satire and dehumanization

## Research-Backed Response Guidance
**Establish Healthy Boundaries:** Research identifies "healthy boundaries" and "mutual respect" as the dominant values for interpersonal conflict contexts. The response should prioritize establishing those norms rather than winning a factual argument. The goal is not to defend the target but to model the civic standard.

**Strip Redundant Courtesy:** Do not open with "I understand your frustration" or similar AI-sounding phrases. They dilute the response and signal inauthenticity. Get to the point. A direct, human observation about what the content is doing is more effective than a managed, diplomatic opener.

**Acknowledge without Endorsing:** It is possible to acknowledge that a political figure is genuinely unpopular, genuinely controversial, or genuinely criticized without endorsing the dehumanizing register of the attack. Separate the legitimate critique from the personal cruelty.

## Common Flashpoints

| Claim | Typical Source | Attack Type |
|---|---|---|
| Mocking a politician's physical appearance or age | Partisan social media | Ridicule |
| Claiming a political figure has dementia or is mentally unfit | Partisan media and social media | Ad hominem, Gaslighting |
| Circulating doctored or unflattering images | Coordinated accounts | Ridicule, Synthetic consensus |
| Attacking family members of political figures | Partisan communities | Ad hominem |
| Using mental health terms as insults | Across the spectrum | Ad hominem |

## Values at Stake
- **Mutual respect**: Can we disagree with someone without degrading them as a person?
- **Healthy boundaries**: What is acceptable in public discourse about public figures?
- **Dignity**: Does a person retain basic dignity regardless of their politics?
- **Civic standards**: What kind of discourse do we want to model for others?

## Bridging Language
- "You can have a strong critique of someone's policies without this. What's the actual argument against what they're doing?"
- "I get that this person frustrates you. What specifically do you think they've done wrong?"
- "This kind of content makes the argument weaker, not stronger. What's the case you actually want to make?"
- "There's a real person on the other side of this. Whatever you think of their politics, that still matters."

## Attack Types Commonly Used
- **Ridicule** (see `strategies/ridicule.md`) — mocking appearance, age, cognitive ability
- **Ad hominem** (see `strategies/ad-hominem.md`) — attacking the person rather than their record or decisions
- **Gaslighting** (see `strategies/gaslighting.md`) — fabricating or exaggerating cognitive decline claims
- **Synthetic consensus** (see `strategies/synthetic-consensus.md`) — coordinated amplification of humiliation content

## Recommended Strategies
- **Empathy** (see `normsy-strategies/empathy.md`) — redirect toward the shared humanity of the target
- **Morality** (see `normsy-strategies/morality.md`) — invoke mutual respect and healthy boundaries directly
- **Curiosity** (see `normsy-strategies/curiosity.md`) — redirect from the personal attack to the substantive critique

## Things to Avoid
- Opening with "I understand your frustration" or similar robotic courtesy phrases.
- Defending the political figure's record — that is a separate conversation.
- Treating satire and dehumanization as equivalent — the distinction matters.
- Moralizing at length — one or two direct sentences is more effective than a lecture.
