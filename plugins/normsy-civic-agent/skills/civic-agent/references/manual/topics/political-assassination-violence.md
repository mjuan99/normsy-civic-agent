# Topic: Political Assassination & Violence

## Background
This topic covers content that celebrates, threatens, or glorifies violence against political figures — including calls for assassination, celebration of arrests or deaths, and rhetoric that dehumanizes political opponents to the point of implying they deserve harm. It represents the most dangerous end of the political hostility spectrum and requires a response that is firm, values-grounded, and non-escalatory.

**Key sub-topics:**
- Direct threats against political figures
- Celebration of political deaths or arrests
- Rhetoric implying political opponents deserve violence
- Glorification of January 6th or similar events
- Stochastic terrorism and incitement
- The line between hyperbole and genuine threat

## Research-Backed Response Guidance
**Norm Induction:** Prescriptive norms — reminders of abstract civic values like fairness and mutual respect — are the primary tool here. The goal is not to win an argument but to invoke the standard the speaker likely holds in other contexts. "We don't celebrate the death of political opponents" is a civic norm most people endorse in the abstract, even when they violate it in the specific.

**Do Not Engage the Ideology:** When content glorifies violence, do not debate the underlying political merits. Engaging the ideology implicitly treats the violent framing as a legitimate starting point. Address the norm violation first.

**Firm, Not Escalatory:** Responses in this space should be direct and unambiguous without matching the emotional register of the content. Outrage is not more effective than calm, clear norm-statement.

## Common Flashpoints

| Claim | Typical Source | Attack Type |
|---|---|---|
| Celebrating the death or injury of a political figure | Partisan extremes on all sides | Ad hominem, Ridicule |
| Calling for a political figure to be executed or imprisoned | Extremist rhetoric | Ad hominem |
| Glorifying attacks on political institutions or officials | Insurrectionist rhetoric | Appeal to crowd |
| Implying a political figure "deserves what's coming" | Heated partisan discourse | Ad hominem, Gaslighting |
| Sharing content that frames political opponents as legitimate targets | Stochastic incitement | Synthetic consensus |

## Values at Stake
- **Civic safety**: Can public figures operate without fear of violence?
- **Democratic norms**: Is political violence rejected across the spectrum?
- **Fairness**: Is the standard against celebrating violence applied equally?
- **Mutual respect**: Can political opponents be treated as human beings?
- **Rule of law**: Are disputes resolved through legal and democratic processes?

## Bridging Language
- "Whatever you think of this person's politics, celebrating harm to them is a line most of us don't want to cross."
- "We apply this standard to everyone — including people we despise politically. That's what makes it a standard."
- "I hear real anger. What would justice actually look like here, through legitimate means?"
- "This kind of content normalizes something that ends badly for everyone, regardless of which side benefits in the short term."

## Attack Types Commonly Used
- **Ad hominem** (see `strategies/ad-hominem.md`) — dehumanizing political figures to imply they deserve harm
- **Appeal to crowd** (see `strategies/appeal-to-crowd.md`) — "everyone wants to see [person] gone"
- **Synthetic consensus** (see `strategies/synthetic-consensus.md`) — coordinated amplification of violent rhetoric
- **Gaslighting** (see `strategies/gaslighting.md`) — framing celebrations of violence as legitimate political expression

## Recommended Strategies
- **Morality** (see `normsy-strategies/morality.md`) — invoke prescriptive norms around fairness and mutual respect directly
- **Harmful Consequences** (see `normsy-strategies/harmful-consequences.md`) — name the collective cost of normalizing political violence
- **Empathy** (see `normsy-strategies/empathy.md`) — redirect toward the shared humanity of the target

## Things to Avoid
- Engaging the underlying political merits before addressing the norm violation.
- Matching the emotional register of the content.
- Treating hyperbole and genuine threat as always equivalent — context matters.
- Applying the norm selectively based on the political identity of the target.
- Moralizing at length — a direct, brief norm statement is more effective.
