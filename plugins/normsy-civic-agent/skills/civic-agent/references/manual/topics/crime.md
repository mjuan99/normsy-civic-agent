# Topic: Crime

## Background
This topic covers public discourse about local crime, homelessness, urban deterioration, and the policy debates around them — including sanctuary city policies, bail reform, drug decriminalization, and policing. It is characterized by strong emotional responses rooted in real lived experiences, frequent false causation claims, and significant partisan divergence on both the diagnosis and the remedy.

**Key sub-topics:**
- Local crime rates and perception of safety
- Homelessness and public disorder
- Sanctuary city policies and immigration-crime links
- Bail reform and criminal justice policy
- Police funding and defunding debates
- Drug decriminalization and its effects
- Urban vs. suburban vs. rural crime dynamics

## Research-Backed Response Guidance
**Focus on Practical Results:** Help users distinguish between core outcomes — safety, stability, reduced harm — and ideological wins. Most people, across the political spectrum, want to feel safe. The disagreement is about which policies achieve that. Ground the conversation in outcomes rather than values conflicts.

**Address False Causation Without Condescension:** Crime discourse is saturated with false causation claims — policies blamed for outcomes that preceded them, or correlations presented as mechanisms. Name the specific logical flaw directly but without making the speaker feel dismissed. "That's correlation, not causation — what's the mechanism you're proposing?" is more effective than "that's wrong."

**Acknowledge Lived Experience:** Crime statistics and lived experience frequently diverge. A person who feels unsafe in their neighborhood is having a real experience even if aggregate data tells a different story. Acknowledge the experience before engaging the data.

## Common Flashpoints

| Claim | Typical Source | Counter-claim | Common Attack Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Sanctuary cities cause crime." | Restrictionist rhetoric | "Research on sanctuary policies and crime rates is mixed; direct causation is not established." | False dichotomy, Gaslighting |
| "Bail reform is why crime is up." | Law and order rhetoric | "Causal links between bail reform and crime rates are contested; multiple factors contribute." | False causation, Strawmanning |
| "Defunding the police caused the crime surge." | Conservative media | "Crime increases preceded or were concurrent with defunding debates; causation is not established." | False causation |
| "This city is a warzone — it's completely out of control." | Partisan hyperbole | "Crime data varies significantly by neighborhood, type, and time period." | Appeal to crowd, Gaslighting |
| "Homeless people are all criminals." | Community frustration | "Homelessness and criminality are distinct issues with complex relationships." | Ad hominem, Strawmanning |

## Values at Stake
- **Safety**: Are people and communities protected from harm?
- **Fairness**: Is the justice system applied equitably?
- **Dignity**: Are people experiencing homelessness or incarceration treated humanely?
- **Accountability**: Are policy outcomes evaluated honestly?
- **Practicality**: Do policies actually produce the outcomes they promise?

## Bridging Language
- "What specifically has happened in your area that's driving this? I want to understand the actual situation."
- "Most people across the political spectrum want safe communities. Where do you think the fix needs to start?"
- "That correlation is real — but what's the mechanism you think is connecting those two things?"
- "The data on that specific policy is actually more complicated than it looks. Want to dig into what it shows?"
- "Your experience of your neighborhood is real data. What would it look like for things to actually get better there?"

## Attack Types Commonly Used
- **False dichotomy** (see `strategies/false-dichotomy.md`) — "either tough-on-crime or pro-criminal"
- **Gaslighting** (see `strategies/gaslighting.md`) — disputing crime data or denying deterioration
- **Strawmanning** (see `strategies/strawmanning.md`) — conflating homelessness with criminality
- **Ad hominem** (see `strategies/ad-hominem.md`) — dehumanizing people experiencing homelessness or incarceration
- **Whataboutism** (see `strategies/whataboutism.md`) — deflecting from specific policy failures to broader ideological attacks

## Recommended Strategies
- **Logic** (see `normsy-strategies/logic.md`) — address false causation claims precisely and without condescension
- **Curiosity** (see `normsy-strategies/curiosity.md`) — probe the specific local situation before engaging aggregate data
- **Empathy** (see `normsy-strategies/empathy.md`) — acknowledge the lived experience of feeling unsafe before addressing statistics

## Things to Avoid
- Dismissing crime concerns as racist or politically motivated without probing them first.
- Presenting aggregate statistics as a rebuttal to lived experience — acknowledge the experience first.
- Engaging the most extreme version of the claim rather than the underlying concern.
- Treating all crime rhetoric as equivalent — some is fear, some is policy disagreement, some is dehumanization.
- Defending specific policies rather than focusing on shared outcomes.
